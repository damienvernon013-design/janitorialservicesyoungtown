Crew Turnover and Why Your Building Keeps Getting New Cleaners

You started with a cleaning crew you recognized. Three months later, you do not know who is in your building. This is the most common complaint about commercial janitorial contractors, and it has a specific cause that most contractors do not explain honestly.

Why Crew Turnover Happens

Janitorial work has high turnover rates across the industry. The reasons are structural: entry-level wages, late-night hours, physical demands, and limited advancement in companies that do not invest in retention. But there is a second driver that is specific to how many contractors are structured: subcontracting.

Many mid-size and larger janitorial companies do not employ the crews that clean your building. They sell the contract, then assign it to a subcontractor — often a small independent operator or a franchise licensee — who hires their own crew. When that subcontractor loses their crew, changes their business model, or loses the assignment, your building gets whoever the contractor can assign next. You were never told because the staffing arrangement was never disclosed.

The solution is simple in principle: use a contractor whose cleaning crews are direct employees covered under the contractor's own payroll and workers' compensation. The crews are more stable, the contractor has more control over training and conduct, and you have a clear accountability chain when something goes wrong.

What Turnover Costs Your Facility

The direct cost of crew turnover is retraining time — and most of that cost lands on you. A new crew member who does not know your building will miss tasks, disturb areas they should not enter, leave lights on in the wrong places, and generate complaints from building occupants who notice the difference from the previous crew. In medical facilities, a crew member unfamiliar with the building layout or disinfection protocol creates a more serious problem than a missed trash can.

The indirect cost is trust. Facilities managers who have been through three crew changes in a year stop trusting that the contractor will maintain quality, start checking the building more often, and eventually put the contract back out to bid — which costs time and procurement budget on top of the cleaning quality problem.

Questions to Ask Before Signing a Contract

  • Are the cleaning crew members your direct employees, or are they subcontracted?
  • What is your annual turnover rate for cleaning staff on contracts similar to mine?
  • If the assigned crew member leaves, what is your replacement process and timeline?
  • Who is the named supervisor for my building, and how long have they been with your company?

A contractor who cannot answer the turnover rate question does not track it — which means they do not manage it.

What Reasonable Looks Like

Some turnover is normal in any service business. What is not normal is a contractor who treats your building as an interchangeable assignment rather than an account with a named crew. A contractor who has been in business for 22 years and direct-hires cleaning staff has built some structural stability into the equation — long tenure at the company level correlates with lower turnover at the crew level, because the management practices that retain customers tend to also retain employees.

When a crew member assigned to your building does leave, you should be notified before the replacement starts — not when you walk in one morning and find someone you do not recognize with a key to your facility.
